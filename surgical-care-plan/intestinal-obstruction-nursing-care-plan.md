<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">Chief Complaints</h3>

<li>Severe abdominal pain — since 1 day.</li>
<li>Abdominal distension — since 1 day.</li>
<li>Vomiting — 5 to 6 episodes.</li>
<li>Failure to pass stool and flatus — since 24 hours.</li>
<li>Nausea and restlessness.</li>
<li>Reduced oral intake and weakness.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">History of Present Illness</h3>
<p>
Mr. Mahendra Singh, a 55-year-old male, was admitted to the emergency ward with complaints of severe abdominal pain, abdominal distension, repeated vomiting and inability to pass stool and flatus for 24 hours.
</p>

<p>
The pain was colicky in nature and gradually increased in intensity. Vomiting was initially food particles and later became bilious. Patient also complained of nausea, restlessness, weakness and reduced oral intake. There was no history of trauma.
</p>

<p>
On examination, abdomen was distended and tender, bowel sounds were exaggerated initially. X-ray abdomen showed multiple air-fluid levels suggestive of intestinal obstruction. Patient was kept NPO, Ryle’s tube was inserted for gastric decompression, IV fluids were started and emergency exploratory laparotomy was planned.
</p>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">Past Medical History</h3>
<ul>
<li>No history of diabetes mellitus.</li>
<li>No history of hypertension.</li>
<li>No history of tuberculosis, asthma or epilepsy.</li>
<li>History of occasional constipation.</li>
<li>No known drug allergy.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">Past Surgical History</h3>
<ul>
<li>History of previous abdominal surgery for appendectomy 10 years back.</li>
<li>No history of previous intestinal obstruction.</li>
<li>No history of blood transfusion reaction.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">Summary of Hospital Stay</h3>
<p>
Mr. Mahendra Singh, a 55-year-old male, was admitted in emergency with complaints of severe colicky abdominal pain, abdominal distension, repeated vomiting and inability to pass stool and flatus for 24 hours.
</p>

<p>
On examination, abdomen was distended and tender. X-ray abdomen showed multiple air-fluid levels suggestive of intestinal obstruction. Patient was kept NPO, Ryle’s tube was inserted, IV fluids and electrolyte correction were started, and emergency exploratory laparotomy was planned after stabilization.
</p>

<p>
Exploratory laparotomy was performed on 21/05/2026 under general anesthesia. Adhesions were released and obstruction was relieved. Post-operatively, patient was monitored for pain, infection, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, drain output, Ryle’s tube drainage, urine output and return of bowel function. Patient improved gradually. Bowel sounds returned, flatus was passed, oral diet was tolerated and wound remained clean and dry.
</p>

📄 Page 15 — Health Education
</h2>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">1. Wound Care</h3>
<ul>
<li>Keep abdominal wound clean and dry.</li>
<li>Do not touch wound with unwashed hands.</li>
<li>Do not remove dressing unless advised.</li>
<li>Observe wound for redness, swelling, discharge, foul smell or wound opening.</li>
<li>Visit hospital for dressing and follow-up as advised.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">2. Medication Advice</h3>
<ul>
<li>Take antibiotics for the full prescribed course.</li>
<li>Do not skip or stop medicines without doctor’s advice.</li>
<li>Take pain medicine only as prescribed.</li>
<li>Take gastric protection medicine before breakfast if prescribed.</li>
<li>Report allergy symptoms such as rash, itching, swelling or breathing difficulty immediately.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">3. Diet Advice After Laparotomy</h3>
<ul>
<li>Start with soft, light and easily digestible diet as advised.</li>
<li>Take small frequent meals instead of heavy meals.</li>
<li>Drink adequate water unless restricted.</li>
<li>Include protein-rich foods such as dal, milk, curd, pulses and eggs if allowed.</li>
<li>Include fruits and vegetables gradually to prevent constipation.</li>
<li>Avoid oily, spicy and gas-forming foods during early recovery.</li>
<li>Do not overeat immediately after discharge.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">4. Activity and Rest</h3>
<ul>
<li>Take adequate rest during recovery period.</li>
<li>Walk slowly at home as tolerated.</li>
<li>Avoid heavy lifting, bending and strenuous work until doctor permits.</li>
<li>Support abdomen while coughing, sneezing or getting up from bed.</li>
<li>Do deep breathing and leg exercises as advised.</li>
<li>Resume normal activities only after medical advice.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">5. Prevention of Complications</h3>
<ul>
<li>Maintain hand hygiene before touching dressing area.</li>
<li>Avoid constipation by taking fluids and fiber-rich foods gradually.</li>
<li>Attend follow-up visits regularly.</li>
<li>Do not ignore persistent vomiting, abdominal distension or severe pain.</li>
<li>Maintain proper nutrition for wound healing.</li>
<li>Avoid self-medication.</li>
</ul>

<h3 style="color:#1a73e8;">6. Warning Signs Requiring Immediate Medical Help</h3>
<ul>
<li>Fever above 100.4°F.</li>
<li>Severe abdominal pain or increasing abdominal distension.</li>
<li>Persistent vomiting.</li>
<li>Inability to pass stool or flatus.</li>
<li>Redness, swelling, pus discharge or foul smell from wound.</li>
<li>Bleeding from wound site.</li>
<li>Wound opening.</li>
<li>Reduced urine output or severe weakness.</li>
</ul>
